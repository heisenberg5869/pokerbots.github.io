analytic

Why the caller tightens

The big blind must call the shove to win the pot. Its break-even equity is the call divided by the pot after calling.

Pot before call — Call — Break-even equity —

As the stack grows, the call gets larger relative to the pot, so the caller needs more equity and calls fewer hands. The shover responds by folding more, until the two ranges meet at indifference. At one big blind the break-even equity is 25%; at twenty it is 48.8%.

mixing

Hands on the boundary

Equilibrium play is often mixed: the same hand shoves some of the time and folds the rest. These are the small blind's mixed hands at the selected depth.

A mixed hand sits at the edge of the range: its two actions are close in value, so small changes in the model can flip it. That is why published charts differ in the margins.

caveats

What these charts assume

  • Both players post one ante, half a big blind each. There are no blinds, so the only asymmetry is acting order, and acting first is a disadvantage.
  • Each deal draws the two hand classes independently, ignoring card removal, so AA against AA is possible.
  • Showdown equity comes from a strength heuristic, not a measured table. Real equities compress the extremes, which tightens both ranges.
  • Every depth is solved for 200,000 chance-sampled iterations at a fixed seed. Hands near a boundary can move a few points under a different seed.

Data: tools/build_pushfold_lab_data.py, 200,000 chance-sampled CFR iterations per depth with a fixed seed. The game posts one ante per player, ignores card removal between the players, and uses a strength-based equity heuristic, so these ranges are wider than published push/fold charts. Treat the shape as the lesson and the boundary hands as approximate.
